How this gets tested

Expect vocabulary and engineering judgment, not CLI commands. Definitions that get tested as vocabulary

learn the nouns

A platform is an integrated collection of capabilities, defined and presented according to the needs of its users. Notice the word that anchors that sentence: users. A platform is a product with internal customers, not an inventory of infrastructure.

The attribute list, worth memorizing

Four terms to be able to define cold

  • Thinnest Viable Platform (TVP): the smallest layer that creates consistency and speeds up delivery, kept intentionally thin. The paper's own minimal example is a wiki page linking to provisioning procedures. Carry this instinct: platform teams build interfaces and experiences, not homemade replacements for managed cloud services or mature upstream projects.
  • Golden path: a templated, well-integrated bundle of code, workflows, and documentation that gets a project moving fast (paved, never mandatory). Our lab implements one end to end in section 3.6: Backstage template → new Gitea repo → ApplicationSet picks it up → live workload.
  • Self-service: a user requests a capability through a portal, API, or CLI and gets it automatically with no human gatekeeper in the loop. Every tool across domain 3 is a different way to deliver that experience.
  • Capability: what the platform actually provides (build automation, observability, secrets, data services), separate from the interface users touch to get it. Scenario questions love testing that distinction.

Capability domains the paper enumerates

Web portals, APIs and CLIs, golden path templates, build and test automation, delivery and verification automation, development environments, observability, infrastructure services, data services, messaging, identity and secrets, security services, and artifact storage. "Which capability domain does this belong to?" is an easy exam question to write, so skim the list until you can attach a concrete tool to every single one.

Maturity, in one line each

The maturity model climbs through four stages: provisional (ad hoc, heroic individual effort) → operational (a dedicated team owns it, fulfilled by request) → scalable (self-service, documented, versioned) → optimizing (measured, funded as a product, continuously improved). If a scenario describes ticket queues and a shared spreadsheet, it's operational at best, and the next step is documented self-service.

The white paper, section by section

the exact lists a vocabulary question draws from

The panel above boils the paper down; this one preserves its exact lists in the paper's order and phrasing, because scenario questions reward the exact nouns. This is CNCF TAG App Delivery's "Platforms White Paper" v1.0, followed by the maturity model in the next panel.

Why platforms: the five benefits

By investing in platforms, the paper says, enterprises can:

  1. Reduce the cognitive load on product teams and thereby accelerate product development and delivery.
  2. Improve reliability and resiliency of products relying on platform capabilities by dedicating experts to configure and manage them.
  3. Accelerate product development and delivery by reusing and sharing platform tools and knowledge across many teams.
  4. Reduce risk of security, regulatory and functional issues by governing platform capabilities and the users, tools and processes around them.
  5. Enable cost-effective and productive use of services from public clouds and other managed offerings by delegating implementations to those providers while maintaining control over user experience.

Why do these benefits compound? The paper's short answer: a small number of platform teams serve many product teams, consolidate shared plumbing, and put user interfaces and experiences first.

The definition, verbatim

"A platform for cloud-native computing is an integrated collection of capabilities defined and presented according to the needs of the platform's users. It is a cross-cutting layer that ensures a consistent experience for acquiring and integrating typical capabilities and services for a broad set of applications and use cases." Platform teams provide capabilities without always implementing them, aiming for "the thinnest reasonable layer that provides consistency across provided implementations", even if version one is just a wiki page linking to provisioning steps.

Platform maturity: five use cases in order

  1. Product developers can provision capabilities on demand and immediately use them to run systems: compute, storage, databases, identities.
  2. Product developers can provision service spaces on demand and use them to run pipelines and tasks, to store artifacts and configuration, and to collect telemetry.
  3. Administrators of third-party software can provision required dependencies like databases on demand and easily install and run that software.
  4. Product developers can provision complete environments from templates combining run-time and development-time services for specific scenarios, such as web development or MLOps.
  5. Product developers and managers can observe functionality, performance and cost of deployed services through automatic instrumentation and standard dashboards.

Attributes of platforms: the seven headings as printed

Platform as a product · User experience · Documentation and onboarding · Self-service · Reduced cognitive load for users · Optional and composable · Secure by default. Watch out for two details people often paraphrase loosely: "User experience" means meeting users where they already work (GUIs, APIs, CLIs, IDEs, and portals tailored to different roles), and "Documentation and onboarding" is where the paper defines a golden path as "an initial project template and documentation" paired with a reusable workflow. "Secure by default" is the briefest of the seven: compliance and validation "based on rules and standards defined by the organization".

Attributes of platform teams

Platform teams own the interfaces and day-to-day experience of using platform capabilities. Their three core jobs:

  1. Research platform user requirements and plan feature roadmap.
  2. Market, evangelize and advocate for the platform's proposed values.
  3. Manage and develop interfaces for using and observing capabilities and services, including portals, APIs, documentation and templates, and CLI tools.

To gather requirements, the paper suggests user interviews, hackathons, issue trackers, surveys, and watching real behavior through observability data. Crucially, a platform team "doesn't necessarily run compute, network, storage or other services". Lean on external or managed capabilities whenever possible, and only build your own when nothing else fits.

Challenges, and enabling platform teams

Three recurring challenges stand out: treating the platform as a product co-designed with users, picking the right initial priorities and partner app teams, and winning executive backing by proving value-stream impact. The paper's advice is practical: bring product managers in from day one, and start with high-demand, undifferentiated capabilities (pipelines, databases, observability) alongside a few eager early-adopter teams who'll champion the platform to everyone else.

To keep the platform team itself from drowning, focus on three levers: build the thinnest viable layer over managed providers, "leverage open source frameworks and toolkits for creating docs, templates and compositions for application team use" (the paper's exact phrasing), and staff the team realistically for its scope and customer count.

Glossary roles

  • Platform capability providers build and run the underlying capabilities the platform exposes (external vendors or internal teams providing infrastructure, runtimes, or supporting services).
  • Platform engineers build and maintain the interfaces and tooling that let application teams provision and wire up capabilities, "according to the requirements and instructions provided by platform product managers".
  • Platform product managers study platform users' pain points, prioritize the roadmap to close gaps, and guide platform teams day to day.
  • Platform teams own the interfaces and experiences (portals, custom APIs, golden path templates), guided by product managers, built by platform engineers, and eventually supported by operators, QA, UX, technical writers, and developer advocates.
  • Platform users are anyone running software on the platform or consuming its capabilities: app developers, operators, data scientists, COTS admins, and information workers.
  • Thinnest viable platform (TVP), coined in Team Topologies (Skelton and Pais): "a careful balance between keeping the platform small and ensuring that the platform is helping to accelerate and simplify software delivery for teams building on the platform."
How this gets tested

Suppose a scenario describes a team running its own Postgres, filing tickets to get namespaces, and working without a roadmap. A complete answer hits the exact paper terms: violated attributes (Self-service, Platform as a product), missing role (platform product manager), the metric that exposes the bottleneck (latency from request to fulfillment), and the capability domain that should absorb Postgres (data services, delegated to a managed provider or operator behind a thin interface). Loose paraphrases leave points on the table.

The maturity model grid

five aspects × four levels, one line each

Where the white paper answers why and what, the CNCF Platform Engineering Maturity Model (v1.0) answers how to grow it. Each aspect is scored independently (an org isn't just "Level 2" across the board), and the model explicitly warns that Level 4 isn't automatically the right target, since every step up costs more budget and effort. Lower levels are tactical; higher levels are strategic.

Aspect (its question)1 Provisional2 Operational3 Scalable4 Optimizing
Investment (how are staff and funds allocated to platform capabilities?)Voluntary or temporary: tiger teams, hack days, burnoutDedicated team: central DevOps or DevEx team funded as a cost center, impact unmeasuredAs product: product management and UX roles, published roadmap, funded by expected value (possibly chargeback)Enabled ecosystem: domain specialists (security, performance) extend the platform without a central backlog
Adoption (why and how do users discover and use platform capabilities?)Erratic: word-of-mouth discovery, every team writes its own scriptsExtrinsic push: mandates and incentives, fragmented usage, office hours as primary supportIntrinsic pull: teams adopt it for the value via self-serve portals and golden paths (willing to pay via chargeback)Participatory: users submit fixes and features through contribution workflows and internal ambassadors
Interfaces (how do users interact with and consume capabilities?)Custom processes: tribal knowledge and manual requestsStandard tooling: documented paved roads and templates that still need expert help, plus template driftSelf-service solutions: one-click provisioning with consistent interfaces and a thin day-2 experienceIntegrated services: capabilities show up natively inside existing tools (IDE, pipeline, default namespaces) and compose cleanly
Operations (how are platforms planned, prioritized, developed and maintained?)By request: reactive work, no maintenance plan, one-off upgradesCentrally tracked: service/owner registry and upgrade burndown lists, still driven manuallyCentrally enabled: standard onboarding for new capabilities, documented upgrades, continuous delivery of the platform itselfManaged services: automated lifecycle with zero user disruption under a shared responsibility model
Measurement (what is the process for gathering and incorporating feedback and learning?)Ad hoc: scattered surveys and anecdotesConsistent collection: standard channels and telemetry, loosely connected to the roadmapInsights: outcomes defined first, metrics picked to track them, product manager owns the feedback loopQuantitative and qualitative: open data, leading indicators, active awareness of Goodhart's law

Try mapping real-world clues onto this grid. "A central team maintains Terraform modules and CRDs that teams copy and tweak" is Interfaces Level 2 (standard tooling, template drift). "An API provisions a database and hands back a connection string and dashboard" is Level 3. "Every new project automatically gets a pipeline space, a namespace, and an OIDC proxy" is Level 4. Those come straight from the model's own examples. Also note that aspects depend on each other: trying to run self-service interfaces (Interfaces 3) on volunteer staffing (Investment 1) is the classic imbalance.

Mental model

Use the white paper for nouns (attributes, capability domains, roles, measures) and the maturity model for verbs (what to improve next, and in which aspect). When a question asks "what's wrong?", reach for the white paper; when it asks "what should they do next?", move one column right in whichever aspect is lagging.

What "API as contract" means when you design one

the part that turns into YAML in 3.2–3.6

Across the rest of domain 3, "designing platform APIs" boils down to five concrete decisions. Measure examples/crossplane/xrd.yaml against this checklist; it's the lab's working example.

  1. Abstraction level. Developers ask for an AppEnvironment with a team name and a quota, not a raw Namespace plus a LimitRange plus two NetworkPolicies. Match the noun already in your user's head. Go too thin and you've added indirection without saving work; go too thick and every edge case demands an escape hatch you forgot to design.
  2. Validate at admission. Use enums, numeric bounds, regex patterns, and CEL rules with human-readable error messages. Typos should fail in the developer's terminal in under a second, not silently in a controller log an hour later.
  3. Report status. Expose clear conditions, phases, and printer columns so consumers can self-diagnose. A platform API without a useful READY column is user-hostile and guarantees your team ends up answering support pings.
  4. Version deliberately. v1alpha1 tells users you're still allowed to break things. Once teams depend on your API, introducing a new served version and a conversion path is the price of evolving it (section 3.2).
  5. Document in the schema. Schema descriptions power kubectl explain directly. That's the documentation developers will actually read, because it lives right inside their terminal.
Design test

Write the 10-line YAML your developer will type before you write a single line of schema. If any of those 10 lines forces the developer to look up an internal implementation detail, your abstraction is leaking. examples/crossplane/xr.yaml shows this done right: a team name and two quota numbers.

Anti-patterns worth naming

  • The platform as a gate. Promises self-service in the slide deck, delivers a ticket queue in reality.
  • The leaky abstraction. Exposes underlying provider details in the API, so every backend migration breaks consumers.
  • The mandated platform. Forces adoption instead of earning it, so teams quietly work around it and you lose the feedback that would make it better.
  • The rebuilt wheel. Writing a custom Postgres operator, CI engine, or secrets store burns your team's time below the line where users don't care, starving the interface work only you can do.

Self-check

vocabulary, out loud
Define a platform in the paper's own terms.

An integrated collection of capabilities, defined and presented according to the needs of its users. That focus on users and presentation (interfaces) is what separates a real platform from a pile of infrastructure.

What is the Thinnest Viable Platform, and what does it imply about build-vs-adopt?

The smallest layer that delivers consistency and speeds up delivery. It pushes you to adopt existing capabilities wherever possible and invest your team's energy in interfaces, integration, and golden paths, the parts nobody can buy off the shelf for your org.

A team can request a namespace through a form, and someone approves it within a day. Is that self-service?

No. Self-service means automated fulfillment with no human in the loop. A web form backed by a manual approver is just a nicer skin over a ticket queue (operational maturity, not scalable). Encode guardrails into the API so fulfillment is automatic, reserving manual approvals for genuine regulatory gates.

Name the DORA four and one platform-specific measure the paper adds.

The DORA four are deployment frequency, lead time for changes, time to restore service, and change failure rate. On top of those, the paper adds platform-specific measures like request-to-fulfillment latency for a capability, time to first contribution, and active adoption, measuring the platform itself rather than just the apps running on it.

Which platform attribute does a mandatory, non-composable platform violate, and why does it matter practically?

"Optional and composable." Practically, mandated platforms hide their own flaws: instead of giving honest feedback that improves the product, teams quietly route around them. Making adoption optional isn't just polite; it's your clearest feedback signal.

List the five benefits the white paper says enterprises get from investing in platforms.

First, reducing cognitive load on product teams to speed up delivery. Second, improving reliability and resiliency by putting dedicated experts on platform capabilities. Third, accelerating delivery by reusing shared tools and knowledge across teams. Fourth, reducing security, regulatory, and functional risk through governance. Fifth, getting more out of public cloud and managed services by delegating implementation while keeping control over user experience.

Name the five aspects and four levels of the Platform Engineering Maturity Model.

The five aspects are Investment, Adoption, Interfaces, Operations, and Measurement; the four levels are Provisional, Operational, Scalable, and Optimizing. Each aspect is scored independently, and the model explicitly notes that Level 4 isn't a blanket goal because every step up costs more budget and time.

Which glossary role decides what the platform should do, and which builds it?

The platform product manager researches user needs, owns the roadmap, and guides the team, while the platform engineer builds and maintains the interfaces and tools to meet those requirements. Capability providers (internal teams or vendors) run the underlying services, and platform users are whoever runs workloads on top.

An organization mandates the platform for production releases and runs office hours because users cannot find features. Which aspect and level?

Adoption at Level 2 (Operational): extrinsic push via mandates and incentives, fragmented usage, and help-desk style office hours. The next rung is Level 3 (intrinsic pull): great docs, ergonomic interfaces, shared roadmaps, and feedback channels that make teams adopt the platform because it genuinely helps them.
