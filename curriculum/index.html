The map

weights from the official blueprint

Exam weight doesn't equal study time. Domains 2 and 3 make up half the score and come down to mechanical reps: the faster your hands move, the higher you score. Domain 4's incident response is the same kind of practice skill. Domain 1 and half of Domain 5 cover ground you may already live in if you run Kubernetes day to day, so test yourself against the exercises first before burning whole evenings there.

Beyond the 29 lessons, you have three ways to test yourself under pressure: two full mock exams (15 timed tasks each across all five domains, zero overlap between papers, real grading scripts, and a built-in 120-minute clock; paper 2 tracks its own score), the drill deck (every self-check question turned into spaced-repetition flashcards weighted toward what you miss), and CNPE Quest, an RPG laid over the whole curriculum where you chat with townsfolk to pick up commands, clear each town's trial (logged straight into your drill history), and debug live faults in dungeons using real CLI commands against a simulated cluster. Every study action (a drill card answered, an exercise verified, a section finished, or a mock task scored) keeps your daily uptime strip green.

The exam

validated as of September 2026

Here's what actually matters from the certification page and the official curriculum PDF:

  • 100% performance-based. You solve real tasks on a remote Linux desktop with a terminal and web UIs. No multiple choice.
  • 120 minutes for 15 to 20 tasks. That gives you roughly 6 to 8 minutes per task, which is why make break puts you on a 7-minute clock. Tasks carry different weights, and the heaviest ones are usually the multi-step builds in Domains 2 and 3.
  • One SSH host per task. You start on a bare base machine (hostname base) with none of the cluster tools installed. Each question's info box tells you which host to ssh into, and you must exit back to base before moving on. Stay on the wrong host and you'll silently score zero with no warning at all. The exam environment stays within a couple of months of the latest Kubernetes minor release (v1.35 with etcd v3.6 at the time of writing), so pay attention to what's running in front of you rather than assuming it matches your cluster at work.
  • Docs are tightly restricted. Far more than most candidates expect. The exam instructions allow kubernetes.io/docs plus the specific links in each task's Quick Reference panel, and block everything else. Tool docs only exist if a task hands you a link. That makes kubectl explain, kubectl api-resources, and --help your fastest, most reliable references, which is why this entire site builds that reflex. Treat the per-section "Docs to know your way around" lists as study-time reading.
  • Example tools listed in the blueprint: Argo, Crossplane, Flagger, Flux, Gatekeeper, Grafana, Istio, Jaeger, Kyverno, Linkerd, OPA, OpenCost, OpenTelemetry, Prometheus, and Tekton. The PDF notes clearly that you won't be quizzed on obscure tool trivia outside the listed competencies.
  • What registration includes: two killer.sh simulator sessions (20 questions each), one free retake, a 12-month eligibility window, and a certification valid for 2 years.
  • Passing score isn't published. Ignore any course that claims a magic cutoff number.
  • The simulator is friendlier than the real test. killer.sh grades your work and is the single best readiness check before exam day, but its allowed-docs list is broader than the real exam's. Practice with your browser restricted to kubernetes.io only, or the real environment will feel like a cold shower.
  • Tools evolve under a live exam. Kyverno 1.17 (February 2026) deprecated classic ClusterPolicy, Policy, and CleanupPolicy in favor of CEL-based ValidatingPolicy, MutatingPolicy, GeneratingPolicy, ImageValidatingPolicy, and DeletingPolicy at v1; Kyverno 1.20 (November 2026) refuses to create or update the legacy kinds altogether. Let the cluster in front of you dictate what you write: learn the new kinds first, and stay fluent enough to read the legacy ones (section 5.2). Same deal for Crossplane v1 vs. v2 (3.5) and Argo CD 2 vs. 3 (2.2): always check the installed version before you start typing.

Read the last sentence first

Exam prompts almost always end with a line like "ensure the application reports Healthy" or "the pod must be rejected." That closing sentence IS the grading check. Read it first, picture the exact command you'll run to verify it yourself, do the work, and run that check before you move on. Both mock exams here follow the exact same pattern so the habit becomes automatic.

How to run a session

five steps, one evening
  1. Spin up just the layers listed in the section's needs bar and tear down what you aren't using. The full stack will make a laptop sweat.
  2. Read the concepts carefully. They're written to be sharp rather than long: the exam tests your hands, not trivia recall, and fast hands need a solid mental model.
  3. Run every exercise and check it off only when its verification passes. If your output doesn't match, debugging why IS the real lesson.
  4. Close the loop with make validate whenever a section calls for it.
  5. Jot down anything you had to look up. That running list is your personal weak-spot map, and it's worth more than any generic study guide.

Study plan

~7 weeks at four sessions a week

Twenty-nine sections plus drills. Speed things up by skimming past any section whose exercises you can already knock out cold. Sequence matters more than pace: finish Domain 2 before Domain 3 (Crossplane and Backstage build right on top of Argo CD), finish Domain 4 before leaning into break drills, and save the mock exams for the end.

Those two killer.sh sessions are the only outside calibration you get before the real exam. Don't burn both right away: use the first one to uncover your gaps, and the second to prove you closed them.

Exam-day tactics

read this again the night before
  • Time-box every task at 7 minutes. Flag it and move on. Getting stubborn on one messy task costs you two easy ones down the line, and the mock exams train this exact discipline.
  • Reach for kubectl explain <kind> --recursive before opening docs. It's faster, and it's guaranteed to match the exact Kubernetes and CRD versions running in front of you.
  • Start any unfamiliar tool with kubectl api-resources | grep <tool>. Every platform tool in this exam is driven through CRDs, and scanning their resource names hands you 80% of the mental model immediately.
  • Verify like the grader does. Don't stop at created or configured. Run get, check status conditions, and test the live behavior.
  • Double-check your SSH host and kubeconfig context. Just like this lab, the exam can spread tasks across multiple hosts and clusters. Run kubectl config get-contexts before touching anything.
  • Read the entire prompt before touching the keyboard. The final sentence is almost always what gets graded.
